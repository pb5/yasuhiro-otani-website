The idea behind this template

A typical song moves forward by changing chord progressions section to section. The track analyzed here is dominated almost entirely by a single 4-bar loop (the chorus alternates between just two chords), and instead builds the song through ① which frequency range each part occupies (spectral zoning) and ② which tracks play in which section (layering tracks in and out). Because this music is designed to run alongside a music video, scene changes are handled by the visuals — so the audio side doesn't need to do it. Every number below is measured directly from stem analysis.

💡 Measurement note An i → III → VII loop is one of the most common patterns in a minor key. Near the end, the whole track was measured shifting up two semitones (into Cm/E♭ territory) — a "final chorus key change." Finish the track without the key change first, then try shifting just the final chorus up +2 semitones if you have the energy for it.

💡 Three techniques found in the measurements ① The synth drops out in the chorus: the synth, which lives in the same 400–1kHz range as the vocal, steps aside when the vocal takes the lead — a real example of resolving frequency conflict through arrangement.
② Bass is completely OFF in the breakdown (measured at −135dB, effectively silent): stripping the low end entirely to maximize the drop into chorus 2.
③ The vocal drops out for a guitar solo in the outro: the guitar pushes forward roughly 8dB in the final 8 bars, taking the seat the vocal's frequency range just vacated.

STEP 4: Widening the vocal (from measured L/R analysis)

🎤 Measured result In the verse, the vocal's L/R correlation is 0.97 — effectively a single center track (even the same material layered onto itself reads as one source at this correlation). In the chorus, L/R correlation drops to 0.3–0.6 with frequency coherence of 0.1–0.3 — meaning genuinely different content sits in the left and right channels. It's not just one take panned both ways; the left and right content actually differs.
🎛 How to reproduce it in this template Verse: melody on a single track, panned center. That's it.
Chorus: duplicate the melody region onto two tracks, pan L100/R100, then make the two sides genuinely different — raise one side 10–20 cents, shift the formant slightly, or record a separate take without disturbing the timing. Any of these works. If both sides stay identical, the image collapses back to center — exactly what the measurements show.

Build order

1
Project setup: BPM 137, 4/4
Six tracks: piano, bass, drums, synth, guitar (optional), melody. Add one more track for the duplicated chorus melody.
2
Build the 4-bar loop: Am | Am | C | G
Piano (whole notes) and bass (root notes) are enough. This is the ground the whole song stands on.
💡 Once it's built, copy-paste it for the entire length of the song. The loop never changes.
3
Copy the basic drum pattern from 1 bar
Follow the grid above. Lower the volume or thin out the intro and breakdown later.
4
Add the eighth-note synth loop
A4→B4→C5→E5. EQ out everything below 400Hz to protect its address.
5
Remove regions to match the arrangement chart
Lay everything out first, then delete the regions marked "—" or "Out" in STEP 2's chart. In particular: pull the synth out of the chorus, and remove bass entirely from the breakdown.
💡 Building a section means deciding what to silence, not what to add.
6
Swap in C | E alternating chords for the chorus only
Replace piano and bass in the chorus section only, alternating C→E→C→E. The E chord is E2+B2+E3+G#3 (bass on E1).
7
Widen the melody in the chorus
Write the melody from the A minor pentatonic, then widen it in stereo for the chorus only, following STEP 4.
8
Finishing: clean up frequencies, and +2 key change if you have energy left
EQ each track to stay within its measured frequency range from STEP 1. Shifting the final chorus up +2 semitones as a whole region matches the behavior of the measured track.

Checklist

No chords used outside the 4-bar loop (Am|Am|C|G), except the chorus's alternating C|E
EQ'd out everything above 400Hz on the bass (matches measurement)
Pulled the synth out of the chorus to make room for the vocal
Removed the bass entirely in the breakdown
Widened the chorus vocal by making left and right genuinely different
Listened through and it didn't feel monotonous despite being one loop
Re-listened to the reference track and confirmed the same moments (synth dropping in chorus, silent bass in breakdown, outro solo)
